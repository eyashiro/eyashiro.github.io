<h3><span style="color:gray">Interdisciplinary project consortia are encouraged, but do EU grant proposal evaluators understand interdisciplinary proposals, or are they just checking boxes?</span></h3>

<span style="color:gray">9 October, 2026</span>
<br />
If we search on the internet, we can find many links to information on how funding agencies promote their evaluation frameworks. We can also find out how they aim to evaluate grant proposals fairly and handle interdisciplinary projects. However, many experts also question the real-world effectiveness of these frameworks.

Importantly, interdisciplinary approaches are becoming increasingly encouraged in EU research and innovation (R&I) funding. And large funding agencies, like the European Commission, encourage applicants in many collaborative calls to form multidisciplinary consortia to tackle big questions addressing today’s problems.

The move towards multidisciplinary perspectives in problem solving is going in the right direction. But a common question people also have is: Do evaluators recruited by the funding agencies actually understand interdisciplinary proposals, or are they just checking boxes off the evaluation sheet?

Recent, dedicated articles to address this question seem to be largely missing. However, systemic problems with proposal evaluation are indeed on many people’s minds.

Although the LinkedIn web article <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/illusion-excellence-critical-examination-eu-system-peter-tom-jones-8vgqe">“The Illusion of Excellence”</a> by Peter Tom Jones, Paul McGuiness, and Kostas Komnitsas critiques EU proposal evaluation in a broader sense, its experienced authors and some commenters also question evaluators’ ability to assess interdisciplinary projects.

## Who is available to review interdisciplinary proposals?
For instance, the authors and comenters in the article argue that paying evaluators €450 per day for their services may favour availability over expertise. Such relatively low pay likely discourages busy leading experts from participating in proposal evaluation. They also argue that many of the experts capable of evaluating these proposals may already be participating as applicants in competing proposals.

Tight deadlines for assessing proposals or evaluators with narrow expertise could also be reasons behind the vague, occasionally off-topic feedback applicants report receiving. For instance, the authors mention receiving Evaluation Summary Reports (ESRs) using formulaic phrases like “not sufficiently developed,” “not convincingly demonstrated,” and “not adequately addressed.” And these comments came without more insights as to why the evaluators decided to say these things. The authors and commenters argue that if evaluators were genuinely knowledgeable about the proposal topics, they may have been able to provide more concrete statements in their reviews.

## Does bringing experts together result in better reviews?
At least some EU-level evaluation panels, such as ERC panels, deliberately bring in experts with a broader understanding of topics to tackle the multidisciplinary nature of proposals. There are typically three or more evaluators assessing each proposal. And the panels may also invite additional evaluators so that interdisciplinary projects are thoroughly reviewed. This framework is itself very well-intended and addresses the challenges of evaluating a diversity of interdisciplinary proposal projects.

But, when discussing RIA, IA, and MSCA evaluations, experts describe consensus meetings as often more focused on rewriting reports to match predetermined scores or to avoid complaints than on scrutinising the proposals themselves. Others wonder whether evaluators are actually assessing impact beyond their own specialist fields.

## What could be causing these problems?
Therefore, the EU Commission does have mechanisms in place to support interdisciplinary reviews. But the discussion in Jones and collaborators’ article and comments suggests a potentially systemic problem. In other words, the inability to obtain consistently high-quality reviews on interdisciplinary proposals seems to remain human- and institution-driven:

* Human-driven: The quality of reviews seems to depend on who reviews the proposals, how much time they have, and what gets discussed during consensus meetings.
* Institution-driven: Institutions in sectors providing evaluators may be encouraging experts to specialise in narrow topics.
